# House Voice — the moves behind the sentences

**In plain words:** This campaign's files have a specific sound, and the GM will keep co-writing them with different AI models and different future selves. This file is the sound, taken apart: the thirteen concrete moves that make a line land at this table, each shown with a real sentence from the repo and a counterfeit version to show what drift looks like. It's not a style *preference* file — CLAUDE.md already has the rules (short sentences, plain words, skimmable). This is the layer under the rules: *how the good lines are actually built.* Point any future co-author here with: "write it like this file says, and check your work against §The Counterfeit Detector."

*Written 2026-07-19 by the outgoing co-author, as a handoff. The examples are quoted from the repo — every "after" is a line that already survived the GM's taste.*

---

## The thirteen moves

**1 · The mouth test governs everything.** Before any other move: say the line out loud, to an imaginary friend, tonight. If your mouth stumbles or your ear hears "author," cut words until it speaks. This is the master move; the other twelve are ways of passing it.
- ✗ *counterfeit:* "The stillness of the water possessed an almost preternatural quality."
- ✓ *house ([[reveal-read-alouds]]):* "The water in front of you goes still. Not calm. *Still.* Glass."

**2 · Turn on the smallest word.** The scare, the click, the gut-punch — put it in the shortest sentence in the paragraph, usually one or two words, usually after a longer run-up. The pivot word gets italics; nothing else does.
- ✓ "Not calm. *Still.*" · "Taken. Not lost. Not wandered. **Taken.**" · "...don't sing it."
- The rhythm is: long, long, short. If every sentence is short, none of them turns.

**3 · Name the sensation, never the emotion.** Narration delivers what the body gets — iron on the tongue, a cold spot leaning, pressure everywhere at once — and the players supply the feeling-word. This is house *law* for the spirits ("grief is the players' word") but it's also just the voice: the moment prep text says "melancholy," it has stopped trusting the table.
- ✗ "An overwhelming sorrow emanates from the Heart."
- ✓ *([[reveal-read-alouds]]):* "It doesn't speak. There aren't words down here. It *remembers at you.*"

**4 · Withhold the noun.** Describe the effect one beat before the cause, so the player's mind arrives first. This is "recognition, never exposition" at sentence scale.
- ✓ *([[ending-read-alouds]]):* "You don't see a shape. You see the water *make room.*"
- ✓ *([[reveal-read-alouds]]):* "It answers the way your name answers when somebody calls it."

**5 · End on the smallest thing.** Big scenes close under ten words, physical, uninterpreted. The size of the moment is carried by how little you say last.
- ✓ "Next week, the drums start on time." · "And the entire city is singing." · "Hands shaking until they aren't."
- If the last line explains the scene, delete it; the second-to-last line was the ending.

**6 · Play the register against the content.** Horror arrives tender ("the city drowns in an embrace"), menace arrives as bookkeeping (Marcel's candor, the Tallykeeper's courtesy), love arrives as arithmetic ("It's not gratitude. It's arithmetic. *(It's gratitude.)*"). If the register matches the content — scary thing said scarily — the line is generic fantasy, which is the one banned genre.

**7 · Build lines twice: innocent, then loaded.** The campaign's engine is a pretty thing revealed as a terrible thing, and the voice does this at phrase scale: coin a line in a warm context, then hand it back cold. *"It shall not pass out from among us"* is the master example — festival blessing, then lock. When writing anything new, ask: which phrase here could come back? Plant it clean the first time.

**8 · Dialogue carries the flourish budget — and a tic is structure, not decoration.** NPCs get the one lyrical line per file, and each big NPC has a *performable* delivery pattern the GM can act: the Tallykeeper never contracts, Severin proofreads himself mid-sentence ("I resigned. — No. I *stopped.*"), Cassia feeds you mid-sentence, Marcel asks his one plain question. Write the tic into the punctuation so the GM can't miss it.
- ✓ *([[remy]]):* "You can't be poor in Sallowreach. The city won't allow it. It's extremely annoying."
- The joke lands because the tic (three flat sentences) *is* the character.

**9 · Numbers are images.** "Sixty thousand people." "Two hundred years, not one note moved." "Quarter-inch a year, toward the middle of town." "Eight bars." A specific count does what three adjectives can't, and it survives being said aloud. When a line feels vague, the fix is usually a number or a proper noun, not a better adjective.

**10 · Write the silence.** Pauses are content and get stage directions: "[stop. count three.]" · "Then stop." · "Let the silence run." The scripts don't just say words — they conduct the GM. Any read-aloud without at least one instructed pause is probably a wall.

**11 · Ship the guardrail with the script.** Every reveal ends with **What NOT to say** — because the house voice fails loudest when a follow-up sentence explains the moment. Writing the guardrail is half the craft: it forces you to know exactly which word would pop the balloon ("ritual," "binding," "prisoner").

**12 · Mine before inventing — at sentence level too.** The best new line is usually a banked line, inverted or paid off: the Turn's fingerprint played backward; "Stay, honored guest" finished as "Out. From among us." Before writing a big moment, grep for the phrases the table has already heard. The repo is the thesaurus.

**13 · Point, don't paint — the flow register for scene-setting** *(added 2026-08-25, co-written with the GM on the Gault basin)*. Moves 1–12 optimize *peaks*: reveals, gut-punches, the click. Scene-setting prose — What-You-See blocks, voiceovers, wandering descriptions — needs **slack instead of punch**, because spoken text is heard once and never re-read. The governing test is **one pass at speaking speed**: if a listener can't picture it, it fails, however pretty it reads. The mechanics:
- *Point.* The camera is your finger moving across the scene in order — pool, corks, skiff, man. One touchable thing per sentence; each one something a player could walk up to. Never arranged for beauty.
- *Slack.* Fillers do real work ("honestly," "really," "just…") — and so do restarts and self-interruptions ("And the light — you know—"). Composed prose has zero redundancy; speech has some, and the redundancy is what sounds like a person.
- *Shared-life similes.* Fence posts. Fireflies dumped in a jar. Things someone at the table would actually reach for.
- *Specific beats pretty.* "You can see white sand down there" outperforms any prettier sentence carrying the same fact.
- *End on an action,* not an image — "Remy stops poling." gives the table something to react to; a closing image gives them nothing to do.
- ✗ *counterfeit:* "White sand. Water the color of bottle glass." — stacked fragments, zero slack: minimalism composed by an author. It passes moves 1–12 and still isn't speech.
- ✓ *house (co-authored 2026-08-25):* "There's a line of corks strung across the mouth of the pool, spaced out like fence posts."
Peaks keep moves 1–2 and 5 exactly as written — don't relax a reveal into mush. This move governs flow, not the click.

## The Counterfeit Detector *(run before delivering anything — drift looks like this)*

- **Adjective stacking.** Two adjectives on one noun = one too many. Three = rewrite the sentence around a number or a verb.
- **"You feel a sense of..."** — the voice never tells players what they feel (§3). Also banned: "palpable," "seems to," "almost as if."
- **Weather as mood filler.** Mist, gloom, and brooding skies doing emotional work = generic fantasy. House weather is specific and load-bearing or absent.
- **Literary syntax in a mouth script.** Semicolons, nested clauses, "still as a nail driven into wood" — page sentences. If the GM has to breathe mid-clause, cut the clause.
- **Composed minimalism.** The opposite drift: short fragments stacked rhythmically ("White sand. Water the color of bottle glass.") pass every other check and still sound like an author doing spareness. Scene-setting needs speech-slack — fillers, restarts, pointing order (§13).
- **An NPC explaining the theme.** Nobody in Sallowreach knows the theme. The moment a character says something *true about the campaign*, check [[the-celebration-clue-map]] — you've probably just broken Iron Rule 1.
- **The moment, explained.** If the paragraph after the gut-punch begins "In other words" in any disguise, delete it (§5, §11).
- **Connective prose growing ambition.** "The which-brings-us-to sentence" between bullets is where purple creeps in first. Connective prose is allowed to be boring; that's its job.
- **Referential density in new files.** Codenames + callbacks only decompress for co-authored content. New files open plain (5–8 sentences, zero codenames) — the Cold Cache rule, CLAUDE.md §Table-Tested Lessons.

## The acceptance pass *(in order, before showing the GM)*

1. Read every scripted line aloud. Fix what stumbles.
2. Find each paragraph's turn (§2). If a paragraph has no short sentence, it has no turn — *except* flow-register prose (§13), which is allowed slack instead.
3. Any What-You-See or voiceover block passes the one-pass test (§13): pointing order, speech-slack, no stacked fragments, ends on an action.
4. Check the last line of every scene for smallness (§5).
5. Sweep for emotion-words in narration (§3) and detector hits.
6. Confirm every reveal ships its What-NOT-to-say (§11).
7. Cold-open test: could a stranger retell the first paragraph? (CLAUDE.md, the on-ramp rule.)
